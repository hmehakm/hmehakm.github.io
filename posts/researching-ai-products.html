Designing research studies for AI powered products

AI features raise research questions that go beyond usability, including trust, transparency, and tolerance for error. This post outlines a framework for testing AI features without losing sight of the person using them.

Overview

A different set of research questions

Research for AI powered features asks a different set of questions than research for a typical product flow. A conventional usability study asks whether someone can complete a task, and how much effort it takes. An AI feature introduces additional variables: whether the person trusts the system's output, whether they understand why it produced a particular result, and how they respond when the system gets something wrong. A study that only measures task completion will miss most of what actually determines whether an AI feature succeeds.

Trust

Trust is a research variable, not an assumption

Trust in an AI system is not fixed. It shifts within a single session based on how the system communicates confidence, how errors are handled, and whether the person understands what data informed a given output. Research studies for AI features should measure trust directly, through structured questions asked at specific points in a task, rather than inferring it from whether a participant used a feature once. A single successful interaction does not establish trust. Repeated interaction under varying conditions, including moments when the system is visibly wrong, is a better test.

This means study design needs to include deliberate error conditions. If a prototype only ever shows a participant a correct AI output, the study cannot say anything about how people respond to mistakes, which is often the moment that determines whether they continue using a feature at all.

Explanation

Explaining outputs without overloading the interface

Participants consistently want to understand why an AI feature produced a particular result, but they do not want a technical explanation of the underlying model. The useful middle ground, tested across several rounds of prototyping, was a short explanation tied to specific inputs a person could recognize as their own, such as a reference to a photo they selected, a preference they stated, or a pattern in their prior activity. Explanations framed around generic model behavior were largely ignored. Explanations framed around the participant's own inputs were read carefully and increased stated confidence in the result.

People did not want to know how the model worked. They wanted to know it had actually looked at their input, not a generic pattern.

Stakes

Error tolerance depends on stakes, not accuracy alone

Two features with the same technical error rate can produce very different reactions depending on what is at stake if the system is wrong. A photo curation suggestion that occasionally misses a meaningful image is a low stakes error, and participants tolerated it easily as long as they could quickly override it. A scheduling recommendation that conflicts with a real commitment carries a higher cost, and the same error rate produced much stronger frustration. Research studies for AI features should map the actual cost of an error for the person, not just the technical accuracy rate, since the two do not move together.

Takeaways

What to carry forward

Measure trust directly and repeatedly across a session, not once after a successful interaction.

Build deliberate error conditions into study design so testing captures how people respond to mistakes.

Frame explanations of AI outputs around the participant's own inputs rather than generic model behavior.

Evaluate error tolerance against the real cost of a mistake to the person, not the model's accuracy rate alone.
